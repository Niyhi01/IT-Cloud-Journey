The structure that makes a reader stop scrolling

Each project page follows the same structure — this isn't new writing, it's selecting your strongest committed artifacts and framing them for someone who's never seen your GitHub history.

1
What it is: 2–3 sentences on the environment — a home lab simulating Tier 1 helpdesk work, a small-business AD domain, or a live AWS cloud support environment.
2
Decision log: 3–5 real decisions with your reasoning, pulled from what you actually chose and why — "why a setgid bit on the Samba share," "why static IP and DNS role before AD DS." This is the highest-signal section — it proves you understand trade-offs, not just steps.
3
Artifacts: your strongest 3–4 tickets from that phase (full 7-step format), the key scripts (bulk-create-users.ps1, backup scripts, stop_all_ec2.sh), and any diagrams (network diagram, VPC diagram).
4
GitHub link: directly to the relevant folder in your repo.
5
"What I'd do differently": 2–3 honest sentences — signals intellectual honesty, a trait employers explicitly look for and rarely see in junior candidates.

Why ATS optimization matters, and how to quantify honestly

Many companies run resumes through an Applicant Tracking System before a human sees them — a single-column, plain-text-friendly layout parses far more reliably than a visually elaborate one. Lead with certifications since they're often the first filter, and quantify project work using your own real numbers, not invented ones.

Vague (avoid)Quantified (use)
"Familiar with troubleshooting""Resolved 24+ documented Tier 1–2 support tickets across hardware, networking, Windows, Linux, and AWS/Azure cloud domains"
"Set up a domain""Built and administered an Active Directory domain with 20 scripted users, 3 verified Group Policy Objects, and a Linux Samba file server"
"Worked with AWS""Deployed and secured a custom AWS VPC with least-privilege IAM, verified security controls against failed-action tests"

List your lab work as real experience, not "personal projects" — a title like "IT Support & Cloud Infrastructure (self-directed technical program)" with the actual months you built it accurately represents 4 months of sustained, hands-on engineering work.

ATS keyword bank

CompTIA A+, AWS Certified Cloud Practitioner, Active Directory, Group Policy, Windows Server, Linux (Ubuntu), PowerShell, Bash, IAM, VPC, DNS/DHCP, VPN, MFA, ticketing, SLA, Tier 1/Tier 2 support, patch management.

D104
The PPA technical brief — a one-page document built specifically for NYSC outreach
What a company deciding whether to accept a corps member actually wants to see, fast
Capstone piece▼
Why this is a separate document from your resume

A resume is written for a hiring process with a job description to match against. A PPA request is different — you're asking a company to take a chance on hosting you as a corps member, often with a shorter, more informal decision process, sometimes through a direct conversation or a single forwarded document. A one-page brief, purpose-built for that moment, converts better than a resume repurposed for it.

The structure — one page, scannable in 90 seconds
1
Who you are, in 2 lines: name, NYSC batch/call-up details, CompTIA A+ and AWS Cloud Practitioner certified.
2
What you can actually do, in 3 bullet points: the single strongest, most concrete claim from each of your 3 environments — e.g. "administered a Windows Server Active Directory domain with scripted user provisioning," "secured a live AWS environment with least-privilege IAM," "resolved 24+ documented Tier 1–2 support tickets."
3
Portfolio link, prominent — a QR code if this will ever be printed, since the reader may not be at a keyboard when they see it.
4
What you're asking for, plainly: a PPA placement in their IT/technical team for your NYSC service year, with your availability dates.

Commit this to your GitHub repo and export it as a clean PDF — you'll use it constantly during Weeks 16–17's outreach.

Foundation▼
The 5 scenarios and where each answer comes from
1
"Walk me through how you'd troubleshoot a user who can't connect to the network." Recite the 6-step methodology, then walk the exact diagnostic sequence from Phase 1's DNS break/fix lab.
2
"How would you onboard a new employee?" Walk through your own Phase 2 onboarding runbook — AD account via your provisioning script, OU placement, GPO-driven drive mapping.
3
"A user says they can't access a shared file, but a coworker in the same role can." Diagnose live: group membership first, then NTFS vs. share permissions — the exact conflict you deliberately created and resolved in Phase 2, Day 42.
4
"How do you handle a request for access to a cloud resource?" Walk through the least-privilege IAM policy from Phase 3 — least privilege by default, verified by a failed-action test, not assumed correct.
5
"Tell me about a time you had to explain something technical to a non-technical person." Use one of your own SOPs or the onboarding runbook as the concrete example — you wrote these to be followed by a stranger, which is exactly this skill in practice.
D106–107
Rehearse out loud until fluent, then Pramp mock interviews
Under 3 minutes per answer, no notes — practiced against a real person, not just a mirror
Practice▼
The practice protocol

Record yourself answering each scenario out loud, without notes, watch the playback critically, and redo it. Then use Pramp.com for free peer mock interviews — a real conversational partner surfaces gaps a solo run-through never will, especially around follow-up questions you didn't anticipate.

D108
The PPA outreach conversation — a different skill from interviewing
Cold-reaching a company to ask them to accept you as a corps member, not respond to a job posting
Foundation▼
Why this needs its own preparation

A job interview responds to a posting where the company already wants to hire someone. A PPA request is you initiating — reaching a company that wasn't necessarily planning to take a corps member this cycle, asking them to say yes to a specific decision with real (if modest) administrative cost to them. The pitch needs to make that "yes" as easy and low-risk as possible.

a cold outreach email/message template — adapt, don't send verbatim
Subject: NYSC PPA request — IT Support / Cloud Support (CompTIA A+, AWS Certified) Hi [Name], I'm an NYSC corps member with hands-on experience in IT support, systems administration, and AWS cloud infrastructure — CompTIA A+ and AWS Cloud Practitioner certified, with a documented portfolio of real environments I built and secured myself (link below). I'm reaching out to ask whether [Company] would be willing to host me as a corps member for my service year, working within your IT/technical team. A quick look at what I can bring: - Active Directory administration, Group Policy, and Windows/Linux server management - AWS cloud support: IAM, VPC networking, security, cost management - 24+ documented technical support cases, from hardware to cloud Portfolio: [link] PPA brief attached (1 page) I'd welcome a short call to discuss further. Thank you for considering it. [Name] · [Phone] · [Email]
COMMON MISTAKE Sending the same generic message to every company without naming a specific person or role. A message addressed to "the IT Manager" found via LinkedIn or the company site, even briefly personalized, gets a materially better response rate than one addressed to a general info@ inbox.

Practice▼
The conversation structure if they say yes to a call
1
A 60-second version of your background and what you built — shorter than an interview answer, since this is a first informal conversation, not a technical screen.
2
Be ready to answer plainly: what would you actually be doing day to day, and are you asking for a paid role or specifically a PPA hosting arrangement — be clear about which, since companies think about these differently.
3
Have your NYSC call-up letter details, service dates, and redeployment/relocation process understanding ready — a company saying yes will often ask practical logistics questions, not just technical ones.

Practice this out loud too, the same way you rehearsed the 5 technical scenarios — a confident, clear 60-second pitch matters as much here as a polished technical answer does in a formal interview.

DAILY
The daily routine — split between PPA outreach and job applications
A repeatable schedule for 9 days, tracked in a spreadsheet every single day
Routine▼
The schedule
Morning — PPA outreach (5–8 companies/day): personalized emails/LinkedIn messages using your Week 15 template, targeting named IT managers or technical leads, not general inboxes.
Mid-day — job applications (10–15/day): LinkedIn Easy Apply, Jobberman, company career pages directly — tailor the resume version per role using your 10-minute process from Week 14.
Evening — LinkedIn engagement: 10–15 connection requests to IT managers and technical leads at target companies, each personalized, plus meaningful engagement on 5 posts in your feed.
Ongoing — 2–3 freelance applications/day on Upwork/Fiverr: "IT support setup," "Active Directory configuration," "AWS environment review" — builds testimonials and cash flow alongside the main hunt.
After 7 days silence: one short, professional follow-up — for both PPA outreach and job applications. Persistence without repetition.
The tracking spreadsheet — separate PPA and job columns, same discipline

Two tabs: one for PPA outreach (company, contact name, date reached out, channel, status, follow-up date), one for job applications (company, role, date, channel, status, follow-up date). Update the moment you act, not from memory at day's end — this is the same discipline as logging a ticket the moment you resolve it.

Reference▼
Where to focus PPA outreach

IT managed service providers (MSPs) are especially strong PPA targets — smaller, technical teams that often welcome extra hands and expose you to varied client environments quickly. Also worth targeting: banks and fintech with in-house IT/infrastructure teams (GTBank, Zenith, Access, Interswitch, Paystack, Flutterwave, Moniepoint), telecoms (MTN, Airtel, Glo — large IT departments), and university/polytechnic IT departments, which frequently host corps members and value the CompTIA A+ and AWS credentials directly.

TARGETS
Target companies and salary ranges — job applications
Realistic entry-level ranges, local and remote
Reference▼
Nigerian market

Small companies and MSPs typically pay ₦80,000–₦150,000/month for Tier 1 support; banks and larger multinationals pay ₦150,000–₦300,000/month; oil & gas and telecoms pay the highest, often ₦250,000–₦450,000+/month for candidates with strong certifications.

International remote

Remote entry-level IT/cloud support roles for candidates outside the US or UK commonly land in the $2,000–$4,000/month range. Prioritize companies explicitly built around distributed support teams — BPOs with a tech focus, MSPs with remote desks, SaaS companies with global support coverage.

Job boards to work daily: LinkedIn (primary), Jobberman (Nigerian market), WeWorkRemotely and RemoteOK (remote-only IT/support roles), and direct MSP career pages.
