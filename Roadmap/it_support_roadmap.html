ROADMAP OVERVIEW
Why this roadmap is built backwards from how courses teach it
Most IT support courses start with slides: OSI model, then a quiz, then the next slide. This roadmap starts with a broken thing in front of you — a PC that won't boot, a user who can't log in, a server with no DNS — and only pulls in exactly the theory needed to fix it. Certifications are checkpoints that prove what you already built, not the goal itself.
1
Break it before you learn to fix it
Every topic starts with a deliberately broken lab — a corrupted boot record, a locked-out user, a misconfigured DNS zone — before any explanation. You diagnose first, read second. This is how real Tier 1/2 support actually works: the ticket arrives broken, nobody hands you the answer.
2
Ticket everything, like a real desk
Every lab task gets logged as a real ticket — company, categorized, prioritized, resolved with a full 7-step write-up (problem, observations, causes considered, tests, root cause, fix, verification). By Month 4 you'll have 24+ real resolved tickets. Quality over quantity: 15 excellent, well-reasoned tickets beat 40 shallow ones.
3
No copy-paste, ever
You type every PowerShell cmdlet, every Bash command, every GPO setting by hand. AI can explain why a command failed. AI does not run the command for you. The muscle memory of the command line lives in your hands or nowhere.
4
Document like you're training your replacement
Every resolved issue gets a short SOP (Standard Operating Procedure) — the exact steps, written so a stranger could follow them cold. This is a real skill gap in junior candidates and one of the fastest ways to stand out in an interview.
5
Certifications prove readiness — labs build it
You sit CompTIA A+ Core 1 and Core 2, and AWS Cloud Practitioner (CLF-C02), only after the hands-on labs that cover their material are done. A cert earned after real practice sticks in an interview; a cert earned by memorizing flashcards falls apart under one follow-up question.
6
PowerShell and Bash are daily tools, not a "scripting week"
Automation — bulk user creation, automated reports, scheduled cleanup — separates a Tier 1 technician from a Tier 2/sysadmin candidate. You start scripting in Month 1 and use it every week after, not as an isolated topic.
7
Git and GitHub start Day 1, not Month 4
Every script, SOP, and ticket gets committed as you build it — starting Week 1, Day 3. By the time the portfolio phase arrives, you're assembling and selecting from 4 months of real commits, not reconstructing your work from memory.

This roadmap runs 4 months — 120 days, split into 4 monthly phases. Each phase builds directly on the last: fundamentals become the foundation a domain server runs on, the domain becomes the environment your cloud account extends, and everything together becomes the portfolio Month 4 turns into job offers.

WEEK 1
Hardware & devices — what's actually inside the box
Motherboard, CPU, RAM, storage, ports/connectors, laptops vs. desktops, mobile devices
Foundation▼
What to understand, not just memorize

CompTIA A+ Core 1 leans heavily on hardware you can see and touch: motherboard form factors and chipsets, CPU sockets and cooling, RAM types and channels, storage (HDD vs SSD vs NVMe and why the interface matters for speed), power supply wattage and connector types, and the full range of ports and cable connectors (USB-C, HDMI, DisplayPort, SATA, PCIe). Laptop-specific components — batteries, displays, docking stations — and mobile device basics (SIM, biometrics, MDM enrollment) round out the domain.

Day 1–2 lab: full hardware inventory via command line, no GUI tools
# Windows — PowerShell, no Device Manager clicking allowed Get-ComputerInfo | Select-Object CsManufacturer, CsModel, BiosVersion Get-CimInstance Win32_Processor | Select-Object Name, NumberOfCores, MaxClockSpeed Get-CimInstance Win32_PhysicalMemory | Select-Object Capacity, Speed, Manufacturer Get-CimInstance Win32_DiskDrive | Select-Object Model, Size, InterfaceType # Linux — same audit, different tools sudo lshw -short lscpu free -h lsblk -o NAME,SIZE,TYPE,MOUNTPOINT

Write this up as your first SOP: "How to audit a workstation's hardware from the command line, Windows and Linux" — a real document a Tier 1 tech would keep pinned. This becomes ticket #1 in your osTicket instance later this week.

COMMON MISTAKE Memorizing port numbers and connector names as flashcards without ever plugging one in. If you have access to any old PC or laptop — even a dead one — open the case, physically identify the RAM slots, SATA ports, and PCIe slots. Tactile memory from 10 minutes with a screwdriver outlasts an hour of flashcards.
Day 3–5: build your home lab — the environment every phase after this runs on
1
Install VirtualBox (free) or VMware Workstation Player (free for personal use) on your main machine.
2
Create 2 VMs: Windows 11 (or Windows 10 if resources are limited) and Ubuntu 22.04 LTS. Allocate at least 4GB RAM and 40GB disk to each if your host machine allows it.
3
Take a clean snapshot of each VM immediately after setup, before installing anything else. This is your "factory reset" button — every lab this month that breaks something on purpose ends with reverting to this snapshot.
4
Install osTicket on a third lightweight VM (or via XAMPP directly on Ubuntu) — this becomes your ticketing system for the rest of the roadmap.
ANALOGY — WHY SNAPSHOTS MATTER A snapshot is a save point in a video game. Real Tier 1 techs get comfortable deliberately breaking test systems because they know exactly how to reset to a known-good state. Without that safety net, you'll hesitate to actually break anything — and hesitation is why tutorial-only learners freeze the first time a real system fails in front of them.
WEEK 2
Networking fundamentals — the domain most techs are weakest in
TCP/IP, DNS, DHCP, subnetting, cabling, wireless, VPN, common ports and protocols
Build▼
Why networking is the domain that separates candidates

Most entry-level candidates can talk about hardware confidently but freeze the moment an interviewer asks "a user says they can't reach the file server — walk me through your diagnostic process." This week fixes that by drilling the exact diagnostic sequence real technicians use, then practicing it against real broken connections you create yourself.

The diagnostic sequence to memorize by doing, not reading
CommandWhat it tells you
ipconfig /all (Win) · ip a (Linux)Does this machine even have a valid IP, gateway, and DNS server?
ping [gateway]Can this machine reach the local network at all?
ping 8.8.8.8Can this machine reach the internet by IP — rules out DNS as the problem.
nslookup [domain]If ping-by-IP works but browsing doesn't, DNS resolution is the suspect.
tracert / tracerouteWhere exactly along the path does the connection stop responding?
netstat -anWhat ports and connections is this machine actually using right now?
Day 6–8 lab: build a broken network, then fix it using only the sequence above
# On your Ubuntu VM, deliberately misconfigure DNS sudo nano /etc/resolv.conf # set nameserver to an invalid IP, e.g. 10.10.10.10 — save and exit # Now diagnose it blind, as if a user just filed this ticket: # "I can't reach any websites, but Slack still shows me as connected" ping 8.8.8.8 # works — so it's not a total connectivity loss ping google.com # fails — confirms DNS is the suspect nslookup google.com # confirms resolution failure, points at bad nameserver # Fix it, document the exact steps, log it as a resolved ticket

Repeat this pattern for at least 3 more scenarios you break yourself: a wrong subnet mask causing a machine to be unreachable on its own subnet, a duplicate IP address conflict, and a firewall rule blocking a specific port. Each one becomes a ticket with a documented resolution.

Subnetting — the one topic worth brute-force practicing

Subnetting is the single most memorization-resistant topic in networking — you either fluently convert CIDR notation to usable host ranges under time pressure, or you don't. Do 15 minutes of subnetting practice problems daily this week (subnettingpractice.com is free) rather than one long session — this is exactly the kind of material that responds to spaced repetition, not cramming.

Day 13–15: break and fix a permissions problem on your Ubuntu VM
# Create a file only root can read, then try to access it as testuser sudo useradd -m testuser sudo -u testuser touch /home/testuser/secret.txt sudo chmod 600 /home/testuser/secret.txt sudo chown root:root /home/testuser/secret.txt # Now, as testuser, confirm the failure and diagnose why su - testuser cat /home/testuser/secret.txt # Permission denied — explain exactly why in your ticket notes # Fix: restore correct ownership exit sudo chown testuser:testuser /home/testuser/secret.txt
COMMON MISTAKE Running every command as root/Administrator "to make it easier." Real environments enforce least privilege, and interviewers ask about it directly. Practice diagnosing and fixing permission problems as the affected user first, escalating to sudo/admin only when the fix actually requires it — and explain in your ticket notes why escalation was necessary.

CompTIA's official troubleshooting methodology — memorize this exact order
1
Identify the problem — gather information, question the user, identify recent changes.
2
Establish a theory of probable cause — start with the obvious, question the obvious if it's wrong.
3
Test the theory to determine the cause — confirm before you act.
4
Establish a plan of action and implement the solution.
5
Verify full system functionality and implement preventive measures.
6
Document findings, actions, and outcomes.

This sequence appears directly on the A+ exam and, more importantly, is exactly the structure a hiring manager wants to hear in an interview answer to "walk me through how you'd troubleshoot X." Use this exact 6-step structure in every ticket you log from here on.

Capstone: 10 resolved Tier 1 tickets in your osTicket instance

Simulate 10 realistic tickets spanning everything from this month — at least 2 hardware-related, 3 networking-related, 3 OS/permissions-related, and 2 security-related. Each ticket must follow the full lifecycle: submitted (written from the user's point of view, non-technical language), triaged (priority + category assigned), diagnosed using the 6-step methodology, resolved with a documented SOP, and closed with a resolution summary.

COMMON MISTAKE Writing tickets that are too easy or too vague ("computer broken, fixed it"). A hiring manager reading these should see real diagnostic thinking — the wrong theory you ruled out, not just the right answer you landed on. A ticket that shows one dead end before the fix is more convincing than one that goes straight to the answer.

WEEK 5
Windows Server fundamentals — installing your first domain controller
Server roles and features, DNS/DHCP server roles, promoting a server to a domain controller
Foundation▼
Why a domain changes everything

Every machine in Month 1 was standalone — local accounts, local policies, nothing shared. A domain is what turns 50 separate computers into one manageable environment: one place to create a user, one place to push a password policy, one place to see who has access to what. Understanding this shift is the conceptual core of the entire month.

1
Download Windows Server 2022 Evaluation (free, 180-day license from Microsoft) and install it as a new VM in VirtualBox — allocate at least 4GB RAM, 60GB disk.
2
Set a static IP on the server VM — a domain controller cannot run on a DHCP-assigned address that might change.
3
Install the DNS Server role first — Active Directory depends on DNS to function, and installing it out of order is one of the most common setup mistakes.
4
Install Active Directory Domain Services (AD DS) role, then promote the server to a new domain controller in a new forest (e.g. corp.lab).
5
Install and configure the DHCP Server role to hand out addresses to future client machines, with a scope matching your lab's private network range.
COMMON MISTAKE Installing AD DS before DNS, or leaving the server on a DHCP-assigned IP. Both cause the domain promotion to silently misbehave in ways that are confusing to debug later — get the static IP and DNS role installed first, every time, on every domain controller you ever build.
WEEK 6
Active Directory deep dive — OUs, users, groups, and Group Policy
Organizational structure, security groups, GPOs enforcing real policy, file share permissions
Build▼
Organizational Units — modeling a real company structure

An OU is a container used to organize users, groups, and computers so Group Policy and delegated permissions can target the right subset of the domain. Model a small company: create OUs for Sales, IT, and Management, each with its own users and, later, its own specific GPOs.

create the OU structure and 5 test users via PowerShell — Active Directory module
New-ADOrganizationalUnit -Name "Sales" -Path "DC=corp,DC=lab" New-ADOrganizationalUnit -Name "IT" -Path "DC=corp,DC=lab" New-ADOrganizationalUnit -Name "Management" -Path "DC=corp,DC=lab" New-ADUser -Name "Amaka Johnson" -SamAccountName "ajohnson" \ -Path "OU=Sales,DC=corp,DC=lab" \ -AccountPassword (ConvertTo-SecureString "P@ssw0rd2026!" -AsPlainText -Force) \ -Enabled $true
Group Policy Objects — enforcing real policy, not just theory

A GPO applies settings automatically to every user or computer in the OU (or domain) it's linked to — password complexity, drive mappings, desktop restrictions, software deployment. This week, build and test 3 real GPOs so the theory has something to attach to.

1
Password policy GPO: enforce minimum length, complexity, and account lockout threshold at the domain level.
2
Drive mapping GPO: automatically map a network drive (e.g. \\SERVER\Sales) for every user in the Sales OU only.
3
Desktop restriction GPO: prevent users in a chosen OU from accessing Control Panel — a common real-world helpdesk policy.

Verify every GPO actually applies: log into a domain-joined client VM as a test user and confirm the policy took effect, using gpresult /r to see exactly which GPOs applied and why. A GPO that "should" work but was never verified is a common source of real production incidents — build the habit of always checking now.

File share permissions — NTFS vs. share permissions

Create a shared folder for the Sales OU and set both share-level and NTFS-level permissions — this is a frequent point of confusion because the two layers combine, and the more restrictive one always wins. Practice diagnosing "I can see the folder on the network but get access denied opening it" — almost always an NTFS permission gap under a share permission that looks fine.

WEEK 7
Linux system administration — the server side, not just the desktop
User/group management, systemd services, package management, SSH, cron, log analysis, firewall
Build▼
Standing up a Linux file server as your domain's Linux counterpart

Most real environments are mixed — Windows domain for user management, Linux servers for specific services. This week builds a Samba-based Linux file server that mirrors the Windows share you built in Week 6, so you understand permission models on both platforms.

Samba file share with proper Linux permission groups
sudo apt update && sudo apt install samba -y # Create a shared group and directory sudo groupadd itshare sudo mkdir -p /srv/samba/it sudo chown :itshare /srv/samba/it sudo chmod 2770 /srv/samba/it # setgid bit — new files inherit the group # Add the share definition to /etc/samba/smb.conf, then restart sudo systemctl restart smbd sudo systemctl status smbd
SSH, cron, and log analysis — the daily sysadmin toolkit

Configure SSH key-based authentication and disable password login entirely — the single most common security hardening step on any Linux server. Set up a cron job that automates a real maintenance task (e.g. nightly log rotation or a disk-space check that emails an alert). Practice reading /var/log/auth.log and /var/log/syslog to answer real questions: who logged in, when, and from where; what service failed and why.

a real cron job — disk space alert, the kind a junior sysadmin actually writes
#!/bin/bash # disk_check.sh — alerts if root partition exceeds 85% usage THRESHOLD=85 USAGE=$(df / | tail -1 | awk '{print $5}' | tr -d '%') if [ $USAGE -gt $THRESHOLD ]; then echo "WARNING: disk usage at ${USAGE}% on $(hostname), $(date)" >> /var/log/disk_alerts.log fi # Schedule it: crontab -e, then add: # 0 * * * * /home/user/disk_check.sh

Finally, enable and configure ufw (uncomplicated firewall) — allow only SSH and Samba ports, deny everything else by default, and verify the rules actually block an unauthorized port using a test connection from another VM.

WEEK 8

Capstone piece▼
Bulk user provisioning — the script that actually separates Tier 1 from Tier 2

Creating one AD user by hand is a helpdesk task. Creating 20 from a CSV, correctly placed into their OUs with the right group memberships, is a sysadmin task — and it's the single clearest automation skill hiring managers look for at this level.

bulk-create-users.ps1 — reads a CSV, creates every user, logs the result
$users = Import-Csv -Path "C:\scripts\new_users.csv" # CSV columns: FirstName,LastName,Department,OU foreach ($u in $users) { $sam = ($u.FirstName.Substring(0,1) + $u.LastName).ToLower() $upn = "$sam@corp.lab" try { New-ADUser -Name "$($u.FirstName) $($u.LastName)" \ -SamAccountName $sam -UserPrincipalName $upn \ -Path "OU=$($u.OU),DC=corp,DC=lab" \ -AccountPassword (ConvertTo-SecureString "Temp@2026!" -AsPlainText -Force) \ -Enabled $true -ChangePasswordAtLogon $true Write-Output "Created: $sam" | Out-File -Append log.txt } catch { Write-Output "FAILED: $sam - $_" | Out-File -Append log.txt } }
Virtualization discipline — snapshots as a real backup strategy

Practice the full cycle: snapshot the domain controller before a risky change (like a GPO that could lock out all users), make the change, verify it, and — deliberately, once — roll back a snapshot to prove you can recover from a mistake cleanly. This exact discipline (checkpoint before risky change) is what separates a sysadmin who causes outages from one who doesn't.

Intro to cloud identity — Azure AD / Intune, the direction the field is moving

Most organizations are migrating identity to the cloud — Azure AD (now Microsoft Entra ID) for authentication and Intune for device management. Create a free Microsoft 365 developer tenant, add a few test users, and compare the experience directly against the on-prem AD you just built: what's the same conceptually (users, groups, policies), and what's different (no domain controller to manage, policies pushed to devices anywhere, not just on the local network).

AWS fundamentals — account, IAM, EC2, S3, VPC basics
Every core service the CLF-C02 exam and real cloud support tickets both depend on
Foundation▼
Account setup, billing alerts, and IAM — in that order, always

Create an AWS Free Tier account. Before touching any service, set a billing alarm at a low threshold (e.g. $5) — this single habit prevents the most common beginner mistake: an accidentally-running resource generating a surprise bill. Then create an IAM user for yourself with least-privilege permissions instead of using the root account for daily work — root is for account-level tasks only, from day one.

create your daily-use IAM user and a billing alarm — AWS CLI
# After installing and configuring the AWS CLI with root credentials once: aws iam create-user --user-name daily-admin aws iam attach-user-policy --user-name daily-admin \ --policy-arn arn:aws:iam::aws:policy/AdministratorAccess aws iam create-access-key --user-name daily-admin # Switch to using these credentials for everything from here on — # never use root access keys for daily work
IAM — users, groups, policies, and the concept every cloud ticket touches

IAM controls who can do what in an AWS account — this is the exact same conceptual model as Active Directory from Month 2, just cloud-native. Create an IAM group called SupportTeam with a policy granting only read access to EC2 and S3, then create a user, add them to the group, and verify the restricted permissions actually work by attempting (and correctly failing) a write operation.

EC2, S3, and VPC — the 3 services every cloud support ticket touches
1
Launch a t2.micro EC2 instance (free tier eligible) running Amazon Linux. SSH into it, confirm you understand security groups as the cloud equivalent of the firewall rules from Month 2.
2
Create an S3 bucket, upload a test file, and practice setting bucket policies — deliberately misconfigure one to be briefly public, observe AWS's warning, then correct it. Understanding accidental public exposure is a real, frequently-tested support scenario.
3
Create a custom VPC with public and private subnets, an internet gateway, and route tables — this is the conceptual foundation for every "why can't this resource reach the internet" ticket you'll ever get.
COMMON MISTAKE Leaving free-tier resources running after the lab session ends. Set a personal rule: stop or terminate every EC2 instance at the end of each study session, and check the AWS billing dashboard weekly without fail. This habit alone prevents almost every "how did I get a surprise bill" story from junior cloud learners.

Build▼
The shared responsibility model — the concept every cloud security question tests

AWS and Azure secure the underlying infrastructure — physical data centers, the hypervisor, network hardware. You (or your employer) are responsible for everything above that: data encryption, access management, security group/firewall rules, and OS-level patching on any VM you run. Almost every cloud security incident in the real world traces back to someone misunderstanding where that line sits — usually an overly permissive security group or a public S3 bucket. Be able to explain this model clearly and give one concrete example on each side of the line.

Basic monitoring — CloudWatch and Azure Monitor

Set up a CloudWatch alarm on your EC2 instance that triggers if CPU usage exceeds 80% for 5 minutes, and an Azure Monitor alert on your VM for the same condition. Generate a brief CPU spike deliberately (a simple stress-test command) and confirm the alarm actually fires — never trust a monitoring config you haven't verified triggers correctly.

The PPA outreach conversation — a different skill from interviewing

A job interview responds to a posting where a company already wants to hire. A PPA request is you initiating — reaching a company that wasn't necessarily planning to take a corps member, asking them to say yes with real (if modest) cost to them. Write a personalized cold-outreach email template naming a specific IT manager or technical lead, not a general inbox, and rehearse a 60-second verbal pitch: who you are, your certifications, what you built, and what you're asking for — a PPA placement with your specific service dates.

Target companies for PPA — IT-heavy Nigerian organizations

IT managed service providers (MSPs) are especially strong PPA targets — smaller technical teams that often welcome extra hands. Also worth targeting: banks and fintech with in-house IT teams, telecoms (large IT departments), and university/polytechnic IT departments, which frequently host corps members and value the CompTIA A+ and AWS credentials directly.

Target companies and salary ranges — Nigerian market

Realistic entry-level ranges based on current 2026 data: small companies and MSPs typically pay ₦80,000–₦150,000/month for Tier 1 support; banks and larger multinationals (GTBank, Zenith, Access, Interswitch, Inlaks, CWG) pay ₦150,000–₦300,000/month; oil & gas and telecoms pay the highest, often ₦250,000–₦450,000+/month for candidates with strong certifications. IT managed service providers (MSPs) are especially good first-job targets — they expose you to many different client environments quickly, accelerating real experience.

Target companies and salary ranges — international remote

Remote entry-level IT/cloud support roles for candidates outside the US or UK commonly land in the $2,000–$4,000/month range depending on employer and time-zone overlap requirements. Prioritize companies explicitly built around distributed support teams (BPOs with a tech focus, MSPs with remote desks, SaaS companies with global support coverage) — these tend to have the most realistic hiring pipelines for candidates applying from Nigeria.

Job boards to work daily: LinkedIn (primary), Jobberman (Nigerian market), Indeed, WeWorkRemotely and RemoteOK (remote-only support/IT roles), and direct MSP career pages — many mid-size MSPs hire faster and with less formal process than large enterprises.

Negotiation — the leverage you actually have

Two real certifications plus three fully documented, portfolio-backed environments (helpdesk, sysadmin, cloud) is a materially stronger position than a candidate with a certification alone and no proof of hands-on ability. Don't accept the first offer reflexively — counter within the realistic ranges above, and ask about certification reimbursement, remote flexibility, and growth path toward Tier 2/sysadmin as negotiable benefits beyond base pay.
